# Strategic Product Thinking, Module 1 Lab (Exercise 1)

## Responses
- **Write one feature from the map that, if an engineer asked “why is this a priority for our 12-week roadmap?”, you honestly could not give a strategic answer for.:** Feature: Leaderboards

I could not honestly justify Leaderboards as a priority for a 12-week roadmap.

If an engineer asked why it matters now, I would not be able to connect it to a specific customer need or business goal. The app's primary purpose is helping users build consistent hydration habits, and there is no evidence that comparing water intake with other users is necessary to achieve that outcome. It also adds complexity around social features, rankings, and privacy.

This highlights the feature-factory trap: just because a feature appears in a mind map does not mean it belongs on the roadmap. Every feature should be tied to a clear customer need and measurable business objective. If I cannot explain why a feature matters, who it helps, and what outcome it drives, it is likely AI-generated noise rather than a strategic priority.
- **Write one feature that looks “correct” for the app but has zero strategic weight, you have no idea if it helps the company make money or whether a single user actually asked for it.:** Feature: Badges

Badges look like a reasonable feature for a water-tracking app, but I cannot justify them as a strategic priority. I do not know whether users actually want them, whether they improve retention, or whether they contribute to revenue. The feature fits neatly into an “engagement” category, but that does not make it valuable. Without evidence that badges solve a real user problem or support a measurable business goal, they are just a well-organized guess. This illustrates that organization is not strategy. A feature should only be prioritized when it is tied to a validated customer need and a desired business outcome.
- **Compare V3 to your Step 1 baseline. What changed, and is the output objectively better, did the AI get smarter, or did your added PM knowledge (JTBD, effort/impact, retention) force it to be useful?:** Compared to the Step 1 baseline, V3 shifted from a simple feature list to a product decision framework. Step 1 generated a broad mind map of possible features, but it did not explain which features mattered, what customer problem they solved, or how they supported business goals. V3 introduced JTBD, user friction points, effort vs. impact prioritization, monetization tradeoffs, retention risks, and strategic tensions. This forced the AI to organize information around customer needs, business outcomes, and tradeoffs instead of simply generating more ideas. The output is objectively more useful, but not because the AI got smarter. The improvement came from adding product management knowledge and frameworks that guided the AI toward more strategic thinking.
- **Key takeaway: how does this prove the PM’s role is to think while the AI’s role is to execute?:** This exercise demonstrates that the PM's value comes from defining the problem, identifying customer needs, evaluating tradeoffs, and deciding what is worth building. The AI can rapidly generate ideas, organize information, and fill in requested structures, but it does not independently determine what is strategically important. Without PM guidance, the AI produces plausible feature lists. With PM guidance, it produces outputs that support decision-making. The PM provides judgment and context; the AI provides speed and execution. In other words, the PM thinks, and the AI executes.
